Python OpenCV Algorithm

A traditional computer vision approach to vehicle segmentation using HSV color thresholding, geometric taillight detection, and GrabCut graph-cut optimization.

Before examining the neural network architectures of YOLO26 and SAM 3, we implemented a traditional computer vision algorithm using Python and OpenCV. Rather than learning features from training data, this algorithm uses explicitly defined rules to locate vehicles and a graph-based optimization algorithm called GrabCut to segment them.

Our implementation consists of two main stages:

  1. Detection (Color Thresholding): Identifies pairs of vehicle taillights using HSV color constraints and vehicle symmetry, providing an approximate Region of Interest (ROI) and vehicle location.
  2. Segmentation (GrabCut): Leverages color relationships and pixel affinities between neighboring pixels to determine which pixels belong to the vehicle versus the surrounding background.

Rule-Based Baseline

Requires zero training data, pre-trained weights, or GPU acceleration. It operates purely on deterministic color and geometric heuristics.

HSV Taillight Detection

Isolates red pixels across dual hue wrap-around ranges (0–12 and 168–179) while filtering out ambient road luminance.

GrabCut Graph Cuts

Optimizes boundary energy by finding the minimum cut in a pixel neighborhood graph, balancing color affinity and region models.

Color Thresholding and Vehicle Detection

The first question the algorithm must answer is: what makes a car a car? To avoid getting too philosophical, try going through the dataset images and keeping note of what similarities between the photos help you find the cars.

Features that stood out the most to us were the symmetry of the back of the car, taillights, and license plates. Various literature strategies examine these visual cues, each with their respective downsides. For the simplicity and reliability of our traditional baseline, our algorithm detects taillights using color thresholding.

Figure 1: HSV color thresholding diagram illustrating the color bounds used to isolate vehicle taillights.

The algorithm begins by converting the image from RGB to HSV (Hue, Saturation, Value) color space. Unlike RGB, HSV separates color identity (hue) from brightness (value), allowing the algorithm to isolate red pixels consistently across varying road lighting conditions.

Because red wraps around the circular HSV hue scale, the algorithm selects pixels within two distinct hue ranges:

$$\text{Hue Range 1: } [0, 12] \quad \cup \quad \text{Hue Range 2: } [168, 179]$$

Pixels falling within either range must also satisfy minimum saturation and brightness thresholds. This produces a binary mask of red objects, isolating candidate taillight regions and defining the approximate bounding box around the vehicle.

Figure 2: Taillight detection via HSV color thresholding and resulting vehicle region proposal.

Image Segmentation with GrabCut

Once the candidate region of interest (ROI) is identified, GrabCut models segmentation as a graph partitioning problem: pixels are nodes in a graph, neighboring pixels are connected by weighted edges, and the algorithm finds the minimum cut that best separates foreground from background.

The intuition behind this graph is illustrated below. Each white circle represents a sampled pixel or small region, and the black edges represent affinities between neighboring pixels. The red and green dashed curves show possible cuts through the graph. A good cut should pass along boundaries where neighboring pixels are dissimilar—such as the border between the car and the road background—rather than through the interior of the vehicle where pixels tend to be more similar. Thus, the preferred cut is the one with the lowest total edge cost.

Figure 3: Min-cut graph partitioning intuition. White nodes represent pixels, black edges represent affinities, and dashed curves represent candidate cuts separating foreground ($S$) from background ($T$).

Formally, the cost of a cut partitioning the graph into foreground $S$ and background $T$ is given by:

$$\text{Cut}(S, T) = \sum_{i \in S, \; j \in T} w(i, j)$$

Here, $S$ is the foreground side of the partition and $T$ is the background side. The minimum cut chooses the partition that minimizes the sum of the weights of all severed edges. Intuitively, cutting a weak connection between dissimilar pixels is cheap, while cutting a strong connection between similar pixels is expensive.

Measuring Color Dissimilarity

To assign these edge weights, GrabCut first measures the color difference between neighboring pixels. If $\mathbf{f}_i$ and $\mathbf{f}_j$ are the color vectors of pixels $i$ and $j$, then their dissimilarity is measured using Euclidean distance in color space:

$$S(\mathbf{f}_i, \mathbf{f}_j) = \sqrt{\sum_k (f_{ik} - f_{jk})^2}$$

Two pixels with similar colors have a small Euclidean distance, while pixels with very different appearances produce a large distance.

Exponential Affinity Weighting

This dissimilarity is converted into an edge weight (or affinity) using an exponential decay function:

$$w(i, j) = A(\mathbf{f}_i, \mathbf{f}_j) = \exp\left( -\frac{\|\mathbf{f}_i - \mathbf{f}_j\|^2}{2\sigma^2} \right)$$

The intuition is straightforward: if two neighboring pixels look very similar, then $\|\mathbf{f}_i - \mathbf{f}_j\|^2$ is small, making $w(i, j)$ close to $1$. This creates a strong edge, making it expensive for the cut to split those pixels apart. If the colors are very different, the exponent becomes more negative and $w(i, j)$ becomes small. Such an edge is cheap to cut, making it a natural place for the segmentation boundary to pass.

The parameter $\sigma$ calibrates the sensitivity: a larger $\sigma$ makes the graph more tolerant of gradual color variations, while a smaller $\sigma$ makes the graph strict, treating subtle color shifts as sharp object boundaries.

Terminal Links and Energy Minimization

In addition to these neighborhood edges, GrabCut connects every pixel to two special virtual terminal nodes: a source node representing the foreground and a sink node representing the background. These terminal connections encode how well each pixel matches statistical foreground or background color models (Gaussian Mixture Models).

GrabCut learns these models directly from the initialization mask: pixels near the detected taillights and in the center of the proposal ROI are marked as likely foreground, while the borders of the ROI are marked as likely background. OpenCV iteratively updates these color models and optimizes the segmentation by minimizing the overall energy:

$$E(\alpha) = U(\alpha) + V(\alpha)$$

The energy formulation balances two complementary objectives:

  • $U(\alpha)$ (Data Term): Penalizes assigning a pixel to foreground or background when its color does not match that region's statistical appearance model.
  • $V(\alpha)$ (Smoothness Term): Penalizes cuts between neighboring pixels that exhibit high affinity, encouraging smooth, natural contours.

The result is a clean binary segmentation mask in which the vehicle body is cleanly separated from the surrounding road, sky, and landscape.

No Training Required

Unlike deep learning models (YOLO26 and SAM 3), the OpenCV pipeline executes purely from handcrafted rules and graph-cut optimization, eliminating the need for extensive training datasets or GPU hardware.

Classical vs. Deep Learning Trade-Off

While fast and lightweight, rule-based detection can struggle with diverse vehicle colors, partial occlusions, and varying taillight shapes—highlighting why modern neural architectures are favored for complex autonomous driving environments.

Energy Minimization Precision

By formulating segmentation as a graph min-cut problem, GrabCut achieves sharp, edge-aligned vehicle boundaries that balance statistical color models against neighborhood smoothness penalties.
